Joinery

Jigsaw puzzles cut from scratch

Joinery makes jigsaw puzzles out of your own pictures, or out of a small gallery of paintings that comes with the app. Up to 300 pieces, on iPhone and iPad.

The gallery leans Impressionist, mostly Monet and Van Gogh and Cassatt with some Winslow Homer, all of it from the open-access collections of the National Gallery of Art, the Art Institute of Chicago, and the Met. They're the collections with scans good enough to hold up when a piece is sitting under your thumb.

The pieces are the point

Most puzzle apps cut on a rigid grid and stamp a handful of tab shapes across it, which is why after twenty pieces they all start to look like each other. Joinery generates every cut from scratch, and no two pieces in a puzzle repeat.

Every cut comes out of an algorithm I wrote, and every rule in it points the same way: it should look like a cut a person could have made, followed with a blade by someone paying attention. So the grid wanders before anything is drawn, and the rows and columns are already crooked by the time the first knob exists. Each knob is then shaped on its own, with its own width, neck, and lean, and they all come out the same size across the puzzle. A knob is the size of the tool that cut it. Scale each one to the piece it sits on, the way a grid-based cut does, and the wide pieces come out fat while the narrow ones look starved. It's the sort of thing you see immediately and can't name.

Where two cuts meet, they don't quite cross. One runs straight through and the other splits, arriving against it at two nearby points instead of one. A die stamps the whole grid at once and its cuts meet in clean plus signs; a blade following a line comes at it crooked. It's the detail I'm proudest of, even though almost nobody will consciously notice it. What they notice is that the pieces look right.

Now and then a cut skips a knob entirely and two pieces meet along a short, nearly straight seam, the way a cutter routing around a tight spot would have done it. On its own, a piece with one of those sides passes for an edge piece, and I've lost a minute at a time walking one around the border before accepting that it goes somewhere in the middle.

All of it is a pure function of one number. The same seed gives back the same puzzle, down to the last control point. The cut above is seed 9, run through the engine the app ships and written straight out as an SVG, so it's the artifact itself rather than a picture of one. It came out at 63 pieces rather than the 64 I asked for, because the engine picks the grid with the squarest cells near the number you give it rather than the one that hits it exactly. Cell shape outranks piece count everywhere in the algorithm.

The pieces have relief. A real cardboard piece keeps a permanent dent around its rim from being pressed out, and Joinery shades every piece to match, lit from one direction, which is why a board reads as objects lying on a table rather than a picture with lines drawn across it. There's no sound in the app. There is a tap when a piece takes, and a piece that closes three joins at once gives you three of them, which is about as close as a slab of glass gets to the sound of cardboard finding cardboard.

You can watch it happen

Making the puzzle is half the app, so it happens in front of you. A plain grid appears. The rows and columns drift off true. Knobs push out along the lines one at a time, and where two lines cross, the junction resolves into its stagger. Then the cut settles and the picture is in pieces.

That isn't a loading animation with the real work hidden behind it. The app is drawing the actual geometry it's about to hand you, stage by stage, from the same record the engine keeps while it cuts. A fresh install has no puzzles in it, on purpose, so the first one you solve is one you watched get cut.

Work however you want

Nothing on the board tells you what to do next. Pieces go wherever you put them and stay there, stacked in a corner or spread out across the table, and a piece only snaps to one it joins. Sort by color, build the border first, or start in the middle with the part you like. Pieces you've joined travel as one, so a section you've built moves in a single drag.

There's no score and no clock. Leave a puzzle half-solved for a month and it comes back exactly as you left it, down to where the loose pieces were lying.

What you do set is the terms, before you start: how many pieces, whether pieces can be turned, and how close one has to land before it snaps home. Three presets cover most people, and all three dials are there if you'd rather set them yourself.

What's not in it

I built this because I wanted a jigsaw app that treated the puzzle as the whole point, and I couldn't find one.

It's free. No ads. No timers, coins, or streaks. No account. Nothing to buy.

It's in beta while I find the things that are still wrong with it. If you run into one, tell me.
